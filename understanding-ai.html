Understanding Artificial Intelligence

Second taught module: July 2026, 12 weeks, 20 credits.
Concepts, history, paradigms and applications of AI, from the legal and ethical frame through machine-learning practice to a delivered AI solution.

Module status
In progress (July 2026 onwards), Units 1–3 released. Done: the first Collaborative Discussion, comprising an initial post, seven peer responses across five threads, and the graded summary post (10% of the module). Next: the second Collaborative Discussion summary (Unit 8), the Individual Essay (Unit 9), and the AI Solution Implementation (Unit 12). Pending: tutor feedback on the first discussion summary, to be quoted verbatim here once released.

Learning outcomes

On completion of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Understand the legal, ethical and professional issues brought up by AI and the impact of AI on society.
  2. Understand and critically analyse the essential concepts, principles, methods, techniques and problems of AI.
  3. Demonstrate a critical understanding of data requirements and programming paradigms applicable to AI.
  4. Apply and evaluate critically the various methods, tools and technologies applied to an AI project in order to develop an effective plan and delivery of solutions to a business problem.

Assessed entirely by coursework, in three components: Collaborative Discussion Summaries (600 words, 300 each, by end of Units 3 and 8, 20%); an Individual Essay on Artificial Intelligence and its Applications (1,750 words, by end of Unit 9, 40%); and an AI Solution Implementation (2,000 words equivalent, by end of Unit 12, 40%).

Graded assessment 1: Collaborative Discussion 1, 10% (Units 1–3)

Why AI is ubiquitous, and why companies should invest. An initial post, seven peer responses across five discussion threads, and a graded summary post. The initial post takes a steel manufacturer running automated rolling mills as its case, arguing that adoption has owed more to data availability than to algorithmic novelty (Banko and Brill, 2001), that industrial AI is an ecosystem investment in infrastructure and expertise rather than software licences (Lee et al., 2018), and that a maintenance-scheduling model and an AI machinery safety component do not attract the same regulatory obligation.

That last claim became the through-line of the whole discussion: obligation should track what a system does, not the fact that AI is involved. Testing it against five peers required four distinct provisions of the EU AI Act, each doing different work:

  • Article 6(1) — the two cumulative conditions for high-risk classification via the Annex I product route, applied first to a machinery safety component and then to diagnostic medical-device software.
  • Annex III, point 5(b) — creditworthiness assessment as high-risk, with fraud detection expressly carved out, which places two banking applications that share data and infrastructure on opposite sides of the line.
  • Article 14 — human oversight as a designed control rather than a final check, including the automation-bias provision, which is the Regulation acknowledging that the human layer can fail because of the model layer.
  • Article 86 — the right to a clear and meaningful explanation of an automated decision, in force since 2 August 2026, which supplies the consequence that the Annex III classification alone does not.

The exchanges did the work the format is meant to do. One peer opened by arguing that safety failures internalise as cost, so efficiency and safety cannot conflict; over four rounds we converged on a layered model in which the layers must fail independently, and he accepted that risk can transfer to workers before it appears in productivity figures. Two others had answered the same question in flatly contradictory ways, one treating governance as a bottleneck and the other as an accelerant, without either acknowledging the other; resolving that tension by putting compliance evidence into the deployment pipeline drew confirmation from a practising cloud solutions architect that the platform capabilities exist and only organisational maturity lags. A fourth thread on employment produced the reading that reinstatement is itself process redesign, so a firm that bolts AI on gets the displacement without the new tasks.

  • Demonstrates: LO1 and LO2 principally, through legal, ethical and professional analysis of AI in a business setting, with LO4 in the investment argument.
  • References: twelve Cite Them Right Harvard sources across the discussion, spanning the module set texts (Russell and Norvig, 2021; Kneusel, 2023), the required reading (Leslie, 2019; Lee et al., 2018; Peres et al., 2020; Kraaijenbrink, 2022), primary legislation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), regulator guidance (Information Commissioner's Office and The Alan Turing Institute), and peer-reviewed economics (Banko and Brill, 2001; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019; Acemoglu et al., 2022; Wamba-Taguimdje et al., 2020).
  • Summary post: 290 words, drawing on all three units, conceding a counterpoint on the regulatory floor in finance and naming purpose drift as the weakness in my own position. Status: complete, all nine components posted. Feedback: to be quoted verbatim once released.

Reflections

Using the 3 W's framework prescribed by the e-Portfolio brief (alternative: Gibbs' Reflective Cycle). The Launch into Computing reflection closed by asking which habits would carry into this module; that question is the natural starting point here.

To be written.
Left deliberately blank. Reflective writing has to be in my own voice to satisfy the University's academic-integrity policy, so it is not drafted for me. Prompts to answer, using the 3 W's structure:
  • What? The first Collaborative Discussion: an initial post arguing that regulatory obligation should follow a system's function rather than its use of AI, seven peer responses testing that claim across manufacturing, healthcare and banking, and a summary drawing on all three units.
  • So what? Three things worth interrogating honestly. First, the position moved: a peer's question reframed value alignment from a technical specification problem into an organisational one, since the risk is not only that a system misreads a goal but that the organisation states the goal too narrowly in the first place. Second, sustained disagreement changed a peer's stated position three times, which says something about what actually persuades in academic exchange as against professional argument. Third, the weakness identified in my own thesis, that classification depends on intended purpose being declarable at design time while purposes drift in production, is a live problem in my day job and not merely an academic caveat.
  • What next? The Individual Essay (Unit 9) and the AI Solution Implementation (Unit 12) both call for the same move on a larger scale. What specifically carries forward, and what should be done differently given that the discussion leaned heavily on legal instruments and comparatively lightly on the technical material in Unit 3?

To be written at module close and revised as a whole against the brief's Distinction criteria before the e-Portfolio is submitted.
