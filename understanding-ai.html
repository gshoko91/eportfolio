Learning outcomes

On completion of this module, students will be able to:

  1. Understand the legal, ethical and professional issues brought up by AI and the impact of AI on society.
  2. Understand and critically analyse the essential concepts, principles, methods, techniques and problems of AI.
  3. Demonstrate a critical understanding of data requirements and programming paradigms applicable to AI.
  4. Apply and evaluate critically the various methods, tools and technologies applied to an AI project in order to develop an effective plan and delivery of solutions to a business problem.

Assessed entirely by coursework, in three components: Collaborative Discussion Summaries (600 words, 300 each, by end of Units 3 and 8, 20%); an Individual Essay on Artificial Intelligence and its Applications (1,750 words, by end of Unit 9, 40%); and an AI Solution Implementation (2,000 words equivalent, by end of Unit 12, 40%).

Units 1–3 / Submitted and graded

Collaborative Discussion 1: why invest in AI?

The first discussion used a steel manufacturer to examine why AI adoption depends on data, infrastructure, expertise and governance. An initial post, seven peer responses across five threads and a summary developed the argument that responsibilities should follow a system's purpose, context and potential harm.

Peer exchanges connected manufacturing with banking, healthcare and employment. They sharpened the distinction between productivity and safety, and identified purpose drift as a weakness in classifying a system only at design time. The discussion examined legal provisions alongside practical governance; it does not establish a compliance assessment for a deployed system.

  • Result: 79% (Distinction), graded on 28 August 2026. The displayed grade is subject to university ratification.
  • Summary: posted 14 August, within the 300-word limit.
  • Feedback: a tutor feedback attachment is available. Its comments have not yet been incorporated into this portfolio.
  • Learning outcomes: LO1 and LO2, with business-planning evidence for LO4.

Units 6–8 / Submitted, awaiting grading at last check

Collaborative Discussion 2: model choice and operational risk

The initial post compared decision trees for process readings with convolutional neural networks for steel-surface inspection. It questioned the assumption that a more complex model automatically replaces a simpler one, and asked when accuracy could justify reduced transparency.

The replies extended that question to pharmaceutical distribution, network intrusion detection and healthcare records. The central development was to examine the complete decision process: reliable labels, meaningful clustering, calibrated confidence, useful explanations and evidence that human review improves the outcome.

What the discussion established

One peer added error consequences and human intervention to her selection criteria after the pharmaceutical exchange. In the steel-mill thread, the responses prompted a more demanding evaluation of oversight, explanations and confidence thresholds. These were reasoned proposals supported by published research; no factory experiment was carried out.

The summary preserves the limits of that evidence: Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone (2024) report an average performance loss relative to the better independent human or AI performance; Adebayo et al. (2018) identify problems with some saliency methods; Guo et al. (2017) provide a reason to check neural-network calibration. None establishes safety in the proposed steel mill.

Unit 6 / Formative outline submitted 7 September

AI investment options for a steel manufacturer

The submitted outline plans a 1,750-word report for a hypothetical EU-based steel manufacturer. It compares classical machine learning, deep learning and large language models as related approaches, with data quality, whole-life costs and the consequences of error shaping the investment decision.

Maintenance planning

Compare regression and tree baselines with sequence models. Evaluate missed failures, useful warning time and unnecessary maintenance.

Surface inspection

Compare a CNN with a simpler image-feature classifier and current inspection practice on held-back production batches.

Technical documentation

Compare retrieval-augmented generation over approved manuals with ordinary document search, including source support and review time.

Feedback requested: whether all three use cases can be developed adequately within 1,750 words, or whether one should be examined in depth. The outline assumes access to suitable data; it reports no company results, budgets or returns.

Earlier reflection: Units 1–3, recorded 15 August

What? — description

Across Units 1–3 I completed the first Collaborative Discussion: an initial post on AI investment in steel manufacturing, seven peer responses across five threads, and a summary within the 300-word limit drawing on all three units. The discussion tested one claim repeatedly: AI governance should follow a system's function and context rather than the fact that AI is involved.

So what? — interpretation

The exchange strengthened the legal and ethical side of my argument, but also exposed its limit. I used the EU AI Act confidently across Articles 6(1), 14 and 86 and Annex III point 5(b), yet the summary forced me to connect that legal frame back to Unit 3's learning-agent material. The key development was recognising purpose drift as a weakness in my own position: a system can move into a new risk context after deployment, so classification at design time is not enough.

What next? — outcome

The next stage is to balance governance analysis with implementation evidence. For the Individual Essay and AI Solution Implementation, I need to show not only why oversight matters, but how data preparation, model evaluation and monitoring decisions make that oversight real. The clearest immediate gap is the missing Python/Jupyter evidence for LO3.

The final module reflection will follow the remaining assessments and tutor feedback. The current evidence connects governance arguments with evaluation design; an implemented solution is still needed to examine those proposals in practice.
